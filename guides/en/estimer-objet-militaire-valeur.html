Valuing a military item: the method, from identification to price

By Augustin, founder of Athena Militaria · Published 23 September 2026

"What is it worth?" is the first question in front of a trunk from the attic, and the one answered worst. Prices are everywhere, and nearly all of them are wrong: asking prices never achieved, guides with no source, valuations made by the person who wants to buy. Here is the method that leads to an honest order of magnitude, whatever the piece.

Why nobody can give you a price from a photograph

There is no official price guide for militaria. No body publishes a scale, and the "price guides" found online are averages of listings, in other words of hoped-for prices. The value of a piece is what a buyer actually paid for a comparable piece, recently.

Two objects that look alike in a photograph can differ in value tenfold. The exact model, the condition, replaced parts, the existence of papers: none of this can be read from a thumbnail. That is why the method starts with identification, never with the price.

Step 1: know exactly what you have

A valuation is only as good as the identification behind it. "A WWI helmet" is not enough: you need the model, the variant, the badge, whether the liner is present. "A medal" is not enough either: you need the exact decoration, its model and its reverse.

The guides devoted to the main families of pieces list the points to check:

  • First World War medals, the Croix de guerre and the Médaille militaire;
  • the Adrian helmet and the German helmet;
  • uniforms, bayonets and insignia;
  • photographs and papers, often overlooked although they change everything.

For anything that looks like ammunition, stop there: it is not a matter of value but of safety, and what to do is set out in old ammunition: what to do.

Step 2: record sales that actually took place

This is the heart of the method. Look for identical or very similar pieces that were sold, with their final price and date.

  • Auction results. Auction houses publish the hammer prices of their past sales, and the large online auction platforms let you search them by keyword.
  • Sold items on listing sites. On the platforms that allow it, filter results on sold items rather than current listings.
  • The Athena Militaria sales archive, which shows the pieces sold on the site, their description and the price achieved.

Record at least five sales where you can, noting the condition of each and what came with it. A single price says nothing: it may come from a heated auction or from a sale with no audience.

What an asking price is not worth

A listing that has been online for six months mainly tells you the price at which the piece did not sell. Asking prices serve at best as a ceiling, never as a reference.

Image search, and its limits

Photographing the piece with Google Lens finds objects that look similar, not yours. The prices displayed are those of listings, often reproductions sold new. It is a good starting point for finding the name of a piece, a poor one for knowing its value.

Step 3: adjust for what makes the difference

From one family of pieces to another, the same factors come back.

  1. Originality. A complete, untouched piece is worth more than one that has been restored, repainted or completed with parts from another. A reproduction has only decorative value.
  2. Condition. Wear from use is normal and accepted; missing parts, active corrosion, moth damage or clumsy repairs bring the price down.
  3. The variant. Within the same model, some variants are far rarer than others, and a single detail often tells them apart.
  4. Documented attribution. A name, a unit, a service history, proven by papers: this is the factor that widens the gap most. An anonymous piece remains one piece among many; the same piece, tied to a man and his story, becomes a document.
  5. The group. One soldier's decorations, papers and photographs are worth more together than apart, and sell as a whole.
  6. Demand. Collectors' tastes change, and some periods or nationalities are more sought after than others. Recent sales reflect this; memories of old prices do not.

The guide to the Adrian helmet applies these criteria one by one and shows how they combine on a specific object.

Step 4: build yourself a range

From the sales you have recorded, set aside the extremes and keep a range: what comparable pieces most often achieve. Then place yours within that range according to what sets it apart, up or down.

Also distinguish the price at which you can sell from the price at which a dealer will resell. A professional who buys to resell has to cover costs and margin: they logically offer less than the final price, and there is nothing dishonest about it. You simply need to know.

The most common traps

  • Valuation by the buyer. Someone who values your piece for free in order to buy it straight away is not a valuer but a buyer. Ask for several opinions.
  • Cleaning the piece before valuation. Polishing, washing or repairing almost always loses value. Present the piece as it was found: see caring for military leather, textile and metal.
  • Papers separated from objects. A certificate or service book sold separately makes both lose their attribution.
  • A reproduction taken for a period piece, and the reverse: see spotting a reproduction or a fake.

When to call on a professional

For a piece that looks rare, a large group or an estate, a professional opinion is justified. Auction houses generally value free of charge the objects they might offer for sale. For a written valuation intended for an estate, a division of property or insurance, go to a specialist expert: this service is paid for, and their independence is exactly what you are buying.

If you sell in France for more than 5,000 euros, be aware that the sale of a collector's item falls under specific tax rules. Check impots.gouv.fr or ask a notary before selling.

Thinking of selling? Listing is free, and your piece is seen by collectors who know what they are looking at. Describe what you see, say what you do not know: that is what sells.

What I cannot tell you here

I cannot give you a price without seeing the piece, and I would be wary of anyone who does so from a description alone. What this guide gives you is the method for not getting it wrong, in either direction.

Photograph the object from every side, markings included, and have it looked at: ask the community. When you are ready, selling military items in France sets out what may be sold freely and what may not.

Frequently asked questions

How can I get a military item valued for free?

First identify the piece precisely, then record sales that actually took place for comparable pieces: auction results, sold items on listing sites, the Athena Militaria sales archive. Auction houses also generally value free of charge the objects they might sell. Be wary of a valuation made by someone who offers to buy straight away.

Is there an official militaria price guide?

No. No body publishes a scale, and the price guides found online are mostly averages of asking prices. The only reliable reference is the price actually achieved by comparable pieces in recent sales.

Can Google Lens value my military item?

No. Image search finds similar-looking objects, often reproductions, and displays listing prices. It helps you find the name of a piece, not its value, which depends on details invisible in a photograph: variant, originality, condition, papers.

Do I have to declare the sale of a military item for tax?

In France, below 5,000 euros, the sale of a collector's item by a private individual does not in principle trigger any specific tax. Above that, specific rules apply: check impots.gouv.fr or ask a notary before selling.
