Inheriting military items: where to start, and in what order

By Augustin, founder of Athena Militaria · Published 9 August 2026, updated 16 September 2026

A trunk in the attic, a uniform in a garment bag, a box of medals at the back of a wardrobe. You did not choose these objects, you do not know their history, and you have no idea whether they are worth anything. The difficulty is not finding information: there is plenty of it, and much of it contradictory. The difficulty is doing things in the right order, because some mistakes made in the first week cannot be undone.

This guide follows that order: make safe, inventory, identify, check what needs checking, and only then decide.

Before anything else: clean nothing, dismantle nothing

It is the most natural reflex and it is the one that costs the most. An old military item draws part of its value from its original condition, including its traces of use.

The six actions that destroy value

  1. Polishing metal. Buffing removes the patina and sometimes the stamps themselves. A belt plate brought to a shine loses part of what made it possible to date it.
  2. Washing a textile. Water sets certain stains, shrinks wool and erases the ink markings applied inside garments, which are often the most useful information the piece carries.
  3. Feeding leather with a modern product. Silicone greases and coloured polishes soak in, darken and stain irreversibly.
  4. Taking a piece apart to look underneath. Original rivets, stitching and fastenings cannot be put back exactly as they were.
  5. Separating a medal from its ribbon, or replacing a tired ribbon with a new one.
  6. Throwing away whatever looks uninteresting. Papers, envelopes, original boxes, booklets, labels. The documentary context is often worth more than the item it accompanies.

The few conservation steps that carry no risk are gathered in caring for military leather, textile and metal.

If a piece is dirty, it will stay dirty for as long as it takes you to understand what it is. Cleaning, when it is justified, is decided after identification, never before.

Putting the pieces somewhere safe in the meantime

Get everything out of the attic and the cellar. They are the two worst places: temperature swings in one case, permanent damp in the other.

Set the items up in a normally heated room, out of direct light. Avoid sealed plastic bags and airtight boxes, which trap moisture and cause condensation. Cardboard, acid-free tissue paper and a cotton cover are enough. Avoid prolonged contact between metal and leather: tannin attacks metals.

Finally, set aside at once anything that looks like ammunition. The section below comes back to this.

Making the inventory, the one step nobody will do for you

Photographing properly

This is what will determine the quality of the answers you go on to get, including at a distance.

Indirect daylight, a neutral plain background. For each piece: an overall view of the front, a view of the back, then sharp close-ups of every marking, stamp, proof mark, label, liner interior, edge and lining. Put a ruler in the frame to give the scale. Photograph what you consider illegible as well: a specialist will often see in it what you cannot.

Writing down what you know of the provenance

Who the item came from, which unit that person served in, where the piece was brought back from, and on what date. A military service record book, a period photograph, a letter, a citation.

Do it now, while the family memory is still within reach. Documented provenance is one of the few things that can never be reconstructed after the event, and it is also one of those that count most to a collector.

Identifying a piece: four questions in order

Which army, which period

Start with the broadest question. The shape of a helmet, the cut of a tunic, the type of buttons and the fastening system generally place a piece within a broad group before any marking has even been read.

Comparing shapes is more effective than searching for words at random. The catalogue is organised by period, which allows exactly that: First World War pieces, Second World War pieces, or the whole catalogue if you cannot yet place the item.

For the pieces found most often in estates, a guide sets out what to look at: First World War medals, the Croix de guerre, the Médaille militaire, the Adrian helmet, uniforms and papers and photographs. The words used by listings and experts are explained in the militaria glossary.

What the markings say

Proof marks, acceptance stamps, sizes, makers' names, numbers, internal labels. Copy them out character by character, without trying to interpret them. Transcribe first, understand afterwards. A single misread letter can point to an entirely different maker, year or country.

Is the whole consistent

A piece assembled from elements of different periods is very common, and not always with any intention to deceive. A helmet fitted with a replacement liner, a tunic with replaced buttons, a medal remounted on a recent ribbon: these are ordinary situations.

Look at the consistency of the wear. An item that was genuinely worn wears in the same places: edges, friction points, areas in contact with the body. Wear that is uniform, or concentrated where there is no reason for it, should draw your attention.

Original, reproduction, or commemorative piece

The historical re-enactment market produces good-quality reproductions, which age and end up turning up in estates without anyone knowing where they came from. Some clues are standard: excessive regularity, modern materials visible on the inside, synthetic thread, a complete absence of markings, or conversely markings that are too crisp on a piece that is otherwise worn.

Doubt is not a problem, a loose assertion is. Have the piece looked at before you write anything in a listing. An exchange with experienced collectors often settles the question in a few messages: ask the community a question.

What must be checked before anything else

Ammunition and pyrotechnic items

Shells, grenades, cartridges, detonators, flares. Do not handle them, do not move them, do not offer them for sale, even if a relative assures you that they are empty or inert.

The procedure is simple: contact the gendarmerie or the police, who will call in the bomb disposal service. The call-out is free and carries no consequences for you. This is the only point in this guide that concerns immediate physical safety.

The steps to follow are set out in old ammunition: what to do.

Firearms, including deactivated ones

French law classifies weapons by category, and the status of deactivated weapons depends on the date of deactivation, the standard applied and the certificate that accompanies it. An old deactivation does not amount to a certificate valid today, and the age of a weapon says nothing about its status.

Our terms of sale set out what is allowed on the site: working firearms of categories A, B, C and D1 that have not been deactivated within the meaning of Implementing Regulation (EU) 2015/2403 may not be offered for sale. Deactivated pieces are accepted subject to the regulations in force, provided they are clearly identified as such in the listing. If you are in doubt about a particular piece, have it confirmed by a gunsmith or by the competent authorities.

Edged weapons

Bayonets, sabres, daggers. Their regime is generally more relaxed than that of firearms, but possession, transport and carrying fall under separate rules: being allowed to own an item does not mean being allowed to transport it freely. Here again, check rather than assume.

Insignia and emblems of dissolved regimes

Some pieces from the 1933-1945 period carry emblems whose wearing and public display are punishable under article R645-1 of the French code pénal, which provides for an exception linked to historical evocation. How that framework applies to possession by a collector and to sale calls for a case-by-case check with a legal professional.

On the site, the rule is explicit: any item glorifying war crimes, crimes against humanity, Nazism or terrorism is barred from sale, as our terms and conditions set out.

In substance, these objects are handled as historical documents. They are described, dated and placed in context. They are not staged and not glamorised. That is the line we apply without exception.

Valuing: understanding the method rather than looking for a figure

There is no official price guide in militaria. Anyone who quotes you a figure without having seen the piece, its markings and its condition is giving you an impression, not a valuation.

Value is built on five elements:

  1. Condition, which weighs more heavily than everything else.
  2. The originality and consistency of the whole, a complete and homogeneous piece being nothing like a recomposed one.
  3. Genuine rarity, which is rarely the rarity the family imagines.
  4. Documented provenance, where it exists.
  5. Current demand for that precise type of piece.

The only workable method is to record sales actually concluded on comparable pieces, of equivalent condition and variant. An asking price is not a market price: it is a request, sometimes maintained for years without a buyer.

The full method, for any type of piece, is set out in valuing a military item.

Lastly, be wary of a valuation given free of charge by the person who wants to buy the item from you. It is not necessarily dishonest, but it is not neutral. For a substantial group, or where an estate is held jointly, a written valuation by an auctioneer or an expert, usually charged for, carries a quite different weight.

Thinking of selling? Listing is free, and your piece is seen by collectors who know what they are looking at. Describe what you see, say what you do not know: that is what sells.

Selling: as one lot or piece by piece

Selling the whole lot to a dealer is quick and simple: one contact, one transaction. The price is necessarily lower, since the buyer has to make a return on the resale.

Before listing anything, check what may be sold freely and what may not: selling military items in France.

Selling piece by piece between collectors takes more time, gives a better return, and has a less obvious advantage: each item goes to someone who knows what it is and will keep it as such.

What makes a listing serious comes down to three points: complete photographs including every marking, a factual description without adjectives, and an explicit statement of what you do not know. Writing “I have not managed to identify this stamp” inspires more confidence than a loose attribution, and protects you in the event of a dispute.

If you do not sell

Keeping the items is a legitimate decision, provided you document it. Write down what you know of the item, who it came from and by what route it reached you, then store that text with the piece. Donation to a local museum or to a military history society is another outcome, often worth considering for documentary pieces.

What gets lost in an inheritance is almost never the object. It is the story that went with it.

Frequently asked questions

I have found a grenade or a shell in an inheritance, what should I do?

Do not handle it, do not move it and do not offer it for sale, even if you have been assured that it is inert. Contact the gendarmerie or the police, who will call in the bomb disposal service. The call-out is free and brings no proceedings against you. Old ammunition remains dangerous, including after several decades.

Should a military item be cleaned before it is sold?

No, and this is the most common mistake. Polishing metal erases the patina and sometimes the stamps, washing a textile sets the stains and erases the internal markings, and a modern care product stains leather irreversibly. A dirty but intact item sells better than one that has been wrongly cleaned.

How can you tell whether a piece is original or a reproduction?

Look at the consistency of the whole rather than at the piece in isolation: materials visible on the inside, type of thread, excessive regularity of the finish, presence or absence of markings, and the distribution of the wear. A piece that was genuinely worn wears at the friction points, not uniformly. If in doubt, have it examined before writing an attribution in a listing.

Can items from the 1933-1945 period be sold in France?

Some of these items circulate legally between collectors, but certain ones carry emblems of dissolved regimes whose wearing and public display are punishable under article R645-1 of the French code pénal, which provides for an exception linked to historical evocation. Applying that framework to sale and to collecting calls for a case-by-case check. The site's terms of sale also prohibit any item glorifying Nazism or crimes against humanity.

How do you value a piece when there is no price guide?

Record sales actually concluded on comparable pieces, of equivalent condition, variant and completeness, rather than asking prices, which reflect nothing more than what the seller wants. The five decisive factors are condition, the consistency of the whole, genuine rarity, documented provenance and current demand. For a substantial group, or an estate to be divided, a written valuation by an auctioneer carries a quite different weight from an informal assessment.

Is it better to sell the lot in one go or piece by piece?

Selling as a single lot to a dealer is quick but is necessarily done at a lower price, since the buyer makes a return on the resale. Selling piece by piece between collectors takes time and a little descriptive work, for a noticeably better return. The choice depends above all on the time you can devote to it and on the number of items involved.
